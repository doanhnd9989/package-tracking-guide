# The "Official Website" Listed for Your Carrier Belongs to Someone Else

**Short answer:** this happens a lot, and on its own it is not evidence that you have been
scammed. The name printed on your parcel is often a *service brand*, not a registered company —
and directories (ours included, until we fixed one) sometimes map that brand to the nearest
business listing they can find. When the mapping is wrong you land on a company that has never
heard of your parcel. Here is how to tell that apart from an actual scam in about two minutes.

---

## Why a shipping label and a company name disagree

A cross-border parcel usually passes through three or four businesses, and only one of them owns
the name you see:

- The **seller** books a line, often through a freight forwarder.
- The **forwarder** creates the number and gives the service a brand name. That brand may be a
  product line, not a legal entity — nobody is obliged to register a trading name as a domain.
- The **line-haul** operator flies or ships it and clears customs.
- A **local carrier** completes delivery, and its scans carry *its* name, not the brand on your label.

So the label on your order can name a service that has no public website at all, while the
company actually operating it trades under a completely different name. Directories paper over
that gap by attaching the closest matching website. That guess is usually fine and occasionally
wrong — and when it is wrong, it is wrong in a way that looks alarming to the person checking.

We hit this on our own carrier directory. One label's listed site turned out to belong to a
business that does not print the label's name anywhere on its own pages. We could not show that
the two were the same operation, so we stopped calling it the carrier's official website rather
than leave an assertion we could not support. If you find a directory that still does, treat the
link as a lead, not a fact.

## The two-minute check

Work down this list. You are testing one question: *does this site claim my parcel's brand?*

1. **Search the site for the label name.** Put the exact brand from your parcel into the site's
   own search, or add `site:` and the brand to a search engine. If the company never writes the
   name anywhere, the directory guessed. That tells you nothing about your parcel's safety.
2. **Does the site even offer tracking?** Many real forwarders sell only to merchants and have no
   consumer tracking box. Absence of one is normal, not sinister.
3. **Compare the city.** Read the first physical scan on your history — a facility and a city.
   If the site is based in the same region, the mapping is plausible. If the scans start in a
   Chinese manufacturing city and the site is a retailer in another country, it is not your carrier.
4. **Check who the number works for.** A forwarder's number frequently stops updating under its
   own brand the moment a local carrier takes over, and the remaining scans arrive under that
   second company's name. A second name near the end of a history is a handover being recorded,
   not a hijack. (See [the handover guide](handover-scan-delivered-to-carrier.md).)

## What is *not* a warning sign

People reasonably but wrongly read all of these as fraud:

- **The site is only in Chinese**, or has no English version. Most forwarders sell to sellers, not shoppers.
- **There is no website at all** for the brand on your label.
- **The company looks like a consultancy or a trading firm** rather than a courier. Forwarding is
  often one service inside a broader logistics business.
- **The scan text switches language mid-history**, or prints an internal code instead of a sentence.
- **Days pass with no new scan** while the parcel is on a plane or in customs. Those two stretches
  produce no events by design.

## What *is* a warning sign

These are about money and urgency, never about a mismatched domain:

- A text, email or WhatsApp message **asking you to pay a fee to release the parcel**, with a link.
  Customs charges are billed by the carrier or the post through their own channels, and no tracking
  page can tell you an amount you owe.
- A "carrier website" that has **a tracking box and a payment form and almost nothing else**.
- A request for **card details, a one-time code, or your ID** to continue a delivery.
- A site that **demands a login before it will show any tracking at all**, reached from a message
  rather than from the seller's order page.

If any of those apply, stop and go back to the shop you ordered from. The retailer is the carrier's
customer and is the only party that can open a case with the line.

## Who to contact, in order

1. **The shop.** Always first. It booked the line, it holds the contract, and it is the only one who
   can ask for an investigation or send a replacement.
2. **The local carrier**, if the last scan already shows a handover and that carrier publishes a
   consumer contact.
3. **The forwarder's brand**, last — and expect no consumer support, because you are not its customer.
