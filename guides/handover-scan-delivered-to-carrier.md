# "Delivered to Final Mile Carrier": Why a Delivery Scan Doesn't Mean Your Parcel Arrived

**Short answer:** on a parcel carried by more than one company, the word *delivered* is written by whoever just finished their own leg — and that leg often ends at a depot, not at your door. Read the **location** on the scan, not the status word.

## Why the word is misleading

A cross-border parcel is rarely carried end to end by one company. A typical chain is:

1. the line the seller booked (collection, consolidation, export),
2. the company that moves it across the border and clears customs,
3. the local post or courier that actually walks up to your address.

Each company scans the parcel when it takes it and when it passes it on. When company 2 passes the parcel to company 3, it records that event in its own system — and from its point of view, it *delivered* the parcel. To its partner.

That is how you end up reading a line like **"Delivered to final mile carrier"** while your parcel is sitting in a sorting centre forty miles away.

Common wordings for the same event:

- Delivered to final mile carrier
- Handed over to local post / handed to delivery partner
- Tendered to delivery service provider
- Transferred to destination carrier
- Delivered to agent / delivered to service point *(see the caveat below — this one is different)*

## The three things "Delivered" can mean

| What the scan says | What actually happened | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Delivered **to a carrier, depot, hub, sorting centre or partner** | Handover between companies. The parcel is still moving. | Nothing. Expect the next scan from the local carrier within a day or two. |
| Delivered **to a shop, locker, access point or parcel shop** | It arrived, but not at your address. It is waiting for you. | Collect it. Most hold points return parcels after about a week. |
| Delivered **at your own town / address**, often with a place note (front door, porch, mailbox, with neighbour) | A real delivery attempt at your address. | If nothing is there, check the property and neighbours, then tell the seller the same day. |

## The ten-second test

Do not read the status word. Read the **location field on the same line**.

- A facility name, a city that isn't yours, an airport code, or another company's name → **handover**.
- A shop name, a locker ID, "access point", "ParcelShop", "service point" → **collect it**.
- Your own town, usually with a place note → **real delivery**.

## Why "delivered to a service point" deserves its own line

This one is not a company-to-company handover and it is not a doorstep delivery either. The parcel is physically finished travelling, it is sitting in a shop or locker, and the clock is running: pickup points typically hold a parcel for about a week and then return it to the sender. It is the only one of the three where waiting can actually lose you the parcel.

## When the silence is normal, and when it isn't

- Handover scan less than two days old → normal. The receiving carrier usually scans within a day or two.
- Handover scan more than a week old with nothing after it → ask the **seller**, not the carrier. The seller holds the shipping contract and can open a query; a recipient normally cannot.
- A delivery scan at your own address with nothing there → check the property, the mailroom and neighbours, then contact the seller **the same day**. Claim windows are short.

## Two numbers, one parcel

After a handover, your national post may issue its own tracking number for the same parcel. Both numbers are valid; they describe different halves of the same trip. If you have both, either one can be looked up — a universal tracker will detect the carrier from the number itself.
