# Your Parcel Is "Returning to Sender" — The Four Reasons, and Which One Hit You

**Short answer:** a return is not a loss. A lost parcel has no scans; a returning parcel has plenty,
which means the network knows exactly where it is. There are only four common causes — the address,
the contents, an unpaid charge, and a holding window that ran out — and the one that applies to you
is almost always written in the two or three scans immediately *before* the direction changed.

---

## Why this is better news than it reads

People see *Return to sender* and assume the worst outcome. Mechanically it is close to the best one
available after something has gone wrong. The parcel is being handled, routed and scanned. That
matters for the thing you actually want, which is your money or a replacement: a parcel that
provably exists and is provably moving back can be refunded, reshipped, and occasionally intercepted.
A parcel with no scans at all is far harder to argue about.

## The four causes

### 1. The address did not work

By a wide margin the most common. A missing apartment or unit number, a postcode that belongs to the
neighbouring suburb, a street name that exists twice in the same city, a business address attempted
after closing. Carriers typically try two or three times and then stop.

**What it looks like:** `delivery attempted`, `unsuccessful delivery`, `no access`, `address
incorrect` or `insufficient address`, dated, just before the reversal. Count the attempts — the dates
tell you whether you got the number of tries the service promised.

### 2. The contents are restricted on that lane

This one catches people out, because the parcel looked ordinary when they bought it. Cross-border
networks publish lines about restricted or prohibited goods. Powders, liquids, aerosols, lithium
batteries, perfumes, some supplements and a long list of unremarkable products are restricted on
particular routes and particular transport modes — the same item can be fine by sea and refused by air.

**What it looks like:** `restricted items`, `prohibited goods`, `item not permitted`, sometimes in the
origin network's own language before any English scan appears. The parcel was *stopped*, not lost, and
no tracking tool can undo it: the decision belongs to customs or to the carrier's compliance rules.

### 3. A duty, tax or handling fee went unpaid

If a charge was raised and nobody paid it, the parcel is not stored indefinitely. The carrier holds
it, sends a notice, and after a set window sends it home.

**The trap:** that notice very often goes to the contact details the *sender* supplied, not the ones
you use. This is why the first many buyers hear of a charge is when their parcel starts travelling
back. If you are expecting anything from abroad, it is worth asking the seller which email address
and phone number they put on the label.

### 4. The holding window ran out

If the parcel went to a pickup point, a parcel locker or a post office counter, it is not kept
forever. These typically hold an item for about a week and then return it. A timeline that shows
arrival at a shop or collection point, then silence, then a return, is usually this — and very often
the card or notification telling you to collect never reached you at all.

## How to find which one applies

Scroll to the point where the direction changed, then read the two or three scans *before* it.
Carriers rarely write a sentence explaining themselves, but they almost always leave a word at the
turning point:

| Word in the scan | Cause |
|---|---|
| attempted, no access, address incorrect | 1 — the address |
| restricted, prohibited, not permitted | 2 — the contents |
| duty, tax, customs charge, fee unpaid | 3 — money |
| unclaimed, not collected, holding period | 4 — the clock |
| refused | the recipient declined it (or someone at the address did) |

## What you can still do

Once a return has been scanned, most networks will not turn a parcel around on the recipient's
request. So spend your effort where it still counts.

1. **Tell the seller immediately**, and include a screenshot of the scan that caused the return.
   They hold the contract with the carrier. That makes them the only party who can request an
   intercept while the window is open, and the party who owes you a refund or a reshipment once it
   has closed.
2. **Keep the message short and factual.** Tracking number, the date and wording of the scan where
   the direction changed, and one clear question: refund or reshipment. Do not ask the seller to
   explain the carrier's decision — they usually cannot, and the thread stalls there.
3. **Note the date the return began.** On most marketplaces and card schemes the buyer-protection
   clock runs from the original delivery estimate, not from the day the parcel finally arrives back
   at the warehouse — and a return leg often takes longer than the journey out.

## What not to do

- Do not keep refreshing in the hope the direction flips back. It does not.
- Do not open a "lost parcel" claim while the tracking clearly shows a return in progress; it will be
  rejected, and it costs you time inside your protection window.
- Do not pay a fee demanded by SMS or email with a link unless you have verified it against the
  carrier's own site. A parcel that is already heading home is a favourite hook for fee scams.
