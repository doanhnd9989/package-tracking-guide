# Your Tracking Page Is in Dutch, German, Spanish or French: The Words That Matter

If your parcel moves through Europe, part of the tracking page will be in the language of whichever network wrote the line. That is not a fault and it does not mean the number is wrong. A tracking page is a log, and each line is written by the system that handled the parcel at that moment.

You do not need the language. On a tracking page there are about six words doing real work, and they repeat across almost every European carrier.

## 1. The word for "tracking"

This is the one that stops people finding the right page at all.

| Language | Word you will see |
|---|---|
| Dutch | Track en trace |
| German | Sendungsverfolgung |
| French | Suivi |
| Spanish | Seguimiento, localizador de envíos |
| Portuguese | Rastreio, seguimento |
| Italian | Tracciatura, tracciabilità |

If a Dutch shop tells you to use *track en trace*, it is asking for nothing more than your tracking number.

## 2. Delivered

The word most people are actually looking for.

| Language | Word |
|---|---|
| Dutch | bezorgd, afgeleverd |
| German | zugestellt |
| French | livré |
| Spanish | entregado |
| Portuguese | entregue |
| Italian | consegnato |

In any language, *delivered* is a scan somebody made, not proof the box is in your hands. The **location** on that line matters more than the word: delivered to your town is a different claim from delivered to a depot, a locker or a pickup point.

## 3. Still moving

| Language | Words |
|---|---|
| Dutch | onderweg |
| German | in Zustellung (closer to "out for delivery"), unterwegs |
| French | en cours d'acheminement, mise en distribution |
| Spanish | en reparto, en tránsito |

Any of these means nothing has gone wrong. Somebody is carrying the parcel and the next scan has not happened yet.

## 4. Customs words, which look worse than they are

*Douane* (Dutch, French), *Zoll* (German), *aduana* (Spanish), *dogana* (Italian).

A customs queue is silent by nature. Days can pass with no new line, and that silence is the most misread thing on any tracking page. See [package held at customs](package-held-at-customs.md).

## 5. One Dutch word worth knowing

**Brievenbuspakje** — letterbox parcel. Something small enough to go through the door slot. It is often marked delivered without anyone ringing the bell, so check the letterbox before assuming it went missing.

## 6. Carrier names are translated too

A carrier can be searched in one language and branded in another:

- **Swiss Post** calls itself **Die Schweizerische Post** ("Die Post") in German.
- **Omniva** is the trading name of **Eesti Post**, the Estonian postal company.
- **PostNL** is the postal and parcel company of the Netherlands, written by customers as *Post NL* and *post.nl* about as often as *PostNL*.

This is the same problem as [carrier name spellings](carrier-name-spellings.md): the name moves, the number does not.

## 7. The last two letters are not the destination

On a 13-character international postal number (two letters, nine digits, two letters), the final two letters say which postal operator **issued** the number — not where the parcel is going. An Omniva number ends **EE** because Estonia issued it, even when it is on its way to you somewhere else.

## If the page still makes no sense

Go to the shop you bought from, not the courier. The shop bought the shipping, so it is the carrier's customer and the one that can open a case. See [who to contact](who-to-contact-parcel-problem.md).

Whatever language the page is in, the thing to watch is the same: the newest line, and how long ago it happened.
