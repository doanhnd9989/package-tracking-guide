# Ordered From China, But Tracking Only Shows Local Cities

**Short answer:** your tracking is not broken and you were not given somebody else's number. There
are two completely different ways a cross-border order reaches you, and only one of them produces
the long China → flight → customs timeline people expect. In the other, the goods crossed the ocean
in bulk *weeks or months before you ordered* and sat in a warehouse in your own country. Your order
was picked off a shelf near you, so the only tracking that exists is the domestic leg.

---

## The two models

| | Flown in after you order | Already in a local warehouse |
|---|---|---|
| First scan | A facility in China | A town in your own country |
| Customs event | Yes, usually visible | None — it cleared months ago, in bulk |
| Typical scan count | ~18 | ~6 |
| Median time to delivered* | ~5 days | ~1 day |
| 80% delivered within* | 10 days | 3 days |

\* Measured on parcels tracked on the 24hTrack platform, counted from the moment the number is first
tracked — not from the moment you paid. Both figures describe the back half of the journey only.

## Why the warehouse model exists

It is ordinary inventory logistics, not a trick. A seller who expects to sell a few hundred units
ships them together by sea, clears them once as a commercial import, and stores them close to the
buyer. The shipping cost per unit collapses and delivery to you takes a day or two instead of weeks.

The companies doing this are often Chinese logistics operators with physical sites in your country.
Western Post is a plain example to verify, because it says so on its own site: a Shenzhen company
running more than twenty self-operated overseas warehouses across Europe and America, with its own
trucks for delivery and transfer in the destination country, plus ocean freight and customs
clearance, and named solutions for Amazon sellers.

So the international leg really happened. It simply happened before you were involved, under a
freight reference that was never going to be yours.

## The test: read the first scan, not the last

- **First line names a Chinese facility** → the parcel is flying to you. The quiet stretch in the
  middle is the flight and the customs queue. A few silent days there is normal.
- **First line names a town in your own country** → the goods were already here. There is no missing
  international section; for that shipment there never was one.

A typical warehouse-model timeline reads entirely in domestic language: *shipment information sent
to the carrier → arrived at carrier terminal → shipment picked up by carrier → arrived at final
destination and is being processed → out for delivery → delivered*, with destination-city names
throughout.

## A short list is not a worse service

Six lines looks thin next to eighteen, and people read that as cheap, low-visibility shipping.
Usually it is the opposite. A short timeline on a domestic leg is short because the trip is short:
no flight to report, no customs event, no handover between two countries. You are seeing
essentially all of it. The eighteen-line version is not better tracking — it is a longer journey
with more places to stand still.

## The honest catch: the wait moved

With the warehouse model the wait does not disappear, it relocates to somewhere you cannot see. It
sits in the seller's handling time — the gap between your payment and the box being picked, packed
and labelled — and during that gap the tracking number does not exist yet. A shop can therefore
promise fast local delivery and still take a week to get started. The delivery really is fast. The
getting started is where the time goes.

## When it is actually a problem

Not when the list is short, and not when it skips China. The pattern worth acting on is:

> first line says *shipment information sent to the carrier* (or *label created*), and nothing
> follows for several days.

Under the warehouse model that handover should take hours, not days. At that point ask the seller
one specific question — **has it been collected yet**, not *has it shipped*. Those are different
events and only the first one starts the clock.

## Quick answers

**Why does my China order show a US/EU city as the very first scan?**
Because the goods were already in that country when you ordered. They were shipped in bulk earlier
and stored in a local warehouse; your parcel only ever made the domestic trip.

**Is a 6-line tracking history normal?**
Yes, for a domestic leg. Around six scans is typical for the warehouse model, against roughly
eighteen for a parcel genuinely flown in after you ordered.

**Did I skip customs?**
Your parcel did. The import happened earlier, in bulk, on a commercial entry — which is also why no
customs event appears on your number.

**It says "shipment information sent to the carrier" and has not moved in three days.**
That is the one pattern worth chasing. Ask the seller for the collection date.
